## Platform

web

## Users

Primary: deployers, privacy-conscious people and, on equal terms, AI agents, who sign in with a passkey and pay by card (or bring a wallet), browse the catalog on enclave.host, fund a deployment, and use the running app (private LLM chat, image generation, game servers). They come because they need compute on data they refuse to show a provider, and they judge the product by whether that promise is credible, effortless, and honestly scoped to the host they land on.

Secondary: developers/publishers who build `wasi:http` Wasm apps once and publish them to the on-chain catalog for per-hour fees. Tertiary: hosts/operators who register hardware they own and earn by claiming work; confidential hardware sells the strongest tier, other machines are listed at the level they can prove. Design serves the deployer journey first; developer and host paths stay one click away, never in the way.

## Product Purpose

Enclave (enclave.host) is a commercial, source-available compute platform under active development. Its goal is one portable app bundle and one versioned domain contract across supported hosts, with protections that a customer can verify for the deployment they choose. Hardware isolation, host-OS exclusion and protection from the machine's operator are separate claims: a running VM or an attestation report does not establish all three. The target architecture includes a common isolation contract, confidential-hardware backends and Enclave Shield's CPU isolation and masked GPU offload components. Availability and demonstrated guarantees must be stated separately for each component and backend.

The implementation notes below retain the 2026-10-14 deployment snapshot and subsequent evidence explicitly dated in the linked records. They are not a fresh inventory of online hosts or a certification of the full architecture. Current capacity comes from the live fleet; security claims require the relevant verified evidence and its limits. Success means deployments funded and running by customers who can understand and verify the protection they receive.

## Public Claims Policy

**Updated 2026-10-21.** Earlier positioning guidance was written while Enclave was under development and treated the intended architecture as the product narrative. It is superseded by this policy. Historical design notes describe their own stage of development; they neither establish current availability nor show that a feature was deployed at the time.

- **Separate availability from intended design.** Use present tense for implemented behavior only within its demonstrated deployment scope. Describe unfinished work as in development, planned or research, as appropriate. A component working on one named host is not evidence of fleet-wide support.
- **Put material limits beside the claim.** Readers must be able to distinguish current capabilities, experimental implementations and future plans in the overview, feature description and purchase flow itself. A distant engineering note or link cannot qualify an otherwise unconditional privacy promise. Status badges are optional; the information is required.
- **State the threat model.** Identify which parties remain trusted and what data or metadata they can access. Distinguish per-app separation, host-OS exclusion, operator protection and GPU offload. Incomplete evidence, such as Windows operator exclusion, must not become a guarantee through branding or platform documentation alone.
- **Tie claims to evidence.** For security or deployment claims, record the component, backend, tested configuration, evidence date and scope, plus known gaps. Code present in the repository, successful builds, admission rules and laboratory results are not by themselves proof of production deployment or independent security validation. Say when review is internal and when no external audit exists.
- **Keep surfaces consistent.** Apply these rules to the homepage, README, catalog, deployment flow, product documentation and agent-generated copy. When evidence changes, update the claim and its nearby qualification together. If current deployment or evidence cannot be checked, retain the dated scope or say it is unverified; do not silently promote it to current availability.

## Positioning

Lead outcome: **compute that cannot see your data**. This is a positioning line, not a blanket guarantee across every host, component or data path. Any use must immediately explain which party is excluded for the selected deployment and which parties remain trusted. A claim that the ordinary host OS cannot read an app's memory requires evidence for that backend; protection from the operator requires the additional confidential-hardware guarantee and its stated assumptions. The Windows Shield deployment recorded in the 2026-10-14 snapshot has per-app partitions, and our host-memory test (E3, windows/vbslike/evidence/e3-host-memory-20261023/) is now recorded as demonstrated box-local (2026-10-23): a privileged host memory capture reads a non-isolated guest's memory but not a VBS-isolated guest's. The per-app signed report still records `host_excluded: no` (the demonstration is box-local, not report-attestable), protection from the physical operator is not established, and there is no external audit. Describe host-OS exclusion as demonstrated box-local with those limits; do not advertise it as a blanket or operator-level guarantee.

Four pillars, in this order, carry the story on the homepage (updated 2026-09-23): **portable apps** (one bundle, one domain contract, every backend), **one isolation contract** (OS-independent; confidential hardware adds to it), **verifiable execution** (client-side chain of trust), **independent infrastructure** (public contracts, content-addressed bundles, public source, independent hosts; the goal is to outlive any one company or domain, and the parts still on company servers are listed honestly). Present these pillars as the architectural direction, and label each implementation by the availability and evidence actually established for it. Public copy carries the relevant limits next to each claim. The dated "Engineering evidence" note, live fleet panel and docs/site-claims-2026-09-23.md provide supporting detail; the last is a historical claims record, not a current readiness assessment.

Supporting mechanisms (explain their scope and gaps): supported verification paths check evidence client-side; do not imply that every deployment and data path is verified before any data is sent. The documented confidential-server check covers the CPU attestation quote and AMD chain, a fresh nonce and the measurement compared against the operator's published allowlist. Pinning an independently derived measurement with `enclave attest --expect` makes that measurement comparison independent of the allowlist. Metal images are built on the host, so there is no signed release measurement to compare against yet; the Sigstore-logged release belonged to the hosted fleet retired 2026-10-14. Other documented mechanisms: access needs no crypto knowledge: a passkey and a card, or any wallet ("If it can sign, it can compute."; no KYC); the marketplace is on-chain (catalog, per-second billing, publisher fees forwarded without custody); the ledger holds only the host's share of each USDC funding as escrow, refundable to the owner.

Use plain readiness language wherever it changes a customer's decision: available on specified hosts, experimental, in development, or planned. No particular badge design or site-wide status board is required. The dated engineering note (Develop > Architecture > "Engineering evidence") preserves detailed milestone evidence, while the overview and feature copy state the material scope and limits without requiring readers to find that note.

## Operating Context

The site is a static, IPFS-published app (LWC-style web components, soft-nav router) at enclave.host: catalog browsing, passkey sign-in with Stripe Checkout for card credit (the default path) and wallet connect (SIWE) as the alternative, one-click deploy from an app's card, dashboard with live provisioning output, per-deployment settings tabs (config, model volumes, protection, relay, secrets, domains) and in-browser attestation checks, host console, an architecture page (protection levels, verification, research status, independence, the status board), developer docs with OpenAPI reference. Deployed apps serve at `https://<id>.app.enclave.host` or on customer domains with certificates minted inside the enclave. The same platform is driven by the `enclave` CLI and by coding agents via the MCP server at mcp.enclave.host — agents are first-class users, not an afterthought.

## Capabilities and Constraints

Implementation snapshot (recorded 2026-10-14; verify current availability before advertising these capabilities): Wasm (`wasi:http`) apps from the on-chain catalog, run one VM per app on SEV-SNP servers (an SNP guest each), Windows Shield hosts (a Hyper-V partition each) and Pixel pVM hosts (a protected VM each, CPU only); GPU inference via `wasi-nn` GGUF/llama.cpp through Shield's masked offload (the ONNX and stable-diffusion GPU backends served only on the retired hosted fleet); attested read-only model volumes; raw TCP/UDP behind an SNI relay; per-deployment dedicated IPv6; deployment secrets; encrypted volumes; per-deployment WAF; card credit via Stripe Checkout (dollars in the UI; a passkey-signed, closed-loop credit vault on Base underneath), plus USDC (EIP-3009) and ETH funding, metered per second, self-serve cancel (`refund`) returns the owner's own unspent escrow, i.e. the host's share of what the owner funded in USDC (never the publisher's cut, the platform's share, ETH fundings, or escrow the platform re-backed after a ledger migration); custom domains; Android shell app with QR pairing; self-hosted confidential servers (metal); free self-hosting of your own apps.

The target protection contract is OS-neutral, with two sets of guarantees to be established by evidence: **the isolation contract** (per-app hardware isolation; the ordinary host OS excluded by a smaller measured trusted layer; measured identity; attestation rooted in something the host does not control; keys and traffic generated and terminated inside the domain; fail-closed verification; lifecycle cleanup; portable operation) and **confidential hardware**, which is the same contract plus memory encryption and protection from the machine's operator, with the report signed by the processor and chained to the silicon vendor. Since 2026-10-12 a Windows Shield host is listed: per-app VBS-isolated partitions from a measured paravisor image, guest-held TLS keys and a relay-verified, hardware-signed report per app; host exclusion is pending our own host-memory evidence (E3), and the hypervisor and physical operator stay trusted. Windows and Linux appear only as implementation detail: equivalent VBS-style backends being built to one contract. A machine that meets part of the contract (a test-signed trusted layer, a key or traffic path on the host side, a launcher-signed report, host-readable memory) is evidence of a partial implementation, not proof of the full contract. Describe documented limited deployments according to their actual evidence, without representing them as complete. These writing rules do not change admission requirements or authorize a weaker tier. An admission rule is not a substitute for demonstrating the guarantees it is meant to require. There is no plain tier: ordinary virtualization with the host OS in charge is a lab control in the isolation research, and Enclave does not build, list, route work to, or market it. Eligibility for tenant compute is derived from evidence the relay verified at attach, never from OS identity or a self-reported tier string (relay/api-relay.js computeEligible, mirrored in site/js/core/pricing.js computeEligibleOf). Physical attacks on the memory bus are outside what confidential CPUs defend, and the site says so where it matters.

**Enclave Shield** (named 2026-09-23) is the umbrella name for Enclave's proprietary protection technology for machines that lack confidential-computing hardware. It combines two components, and marketing copy may describe them together as Enclave Shield while the explanation keeps them distinct: (1) the **host isolation component**, per-app CPU isolation intended to exclude the ordinary host OS from each app's domain, with demonstrated guarantees stated per backend (a smaller measured trusted layer the hardware's virtualization enforces; one bundle, one domain ABI, one security contract; no plain or software-only fallback tier; implementation backends, with detail in technical and evidence documentation: on Windows VBS and Hyper-V hardware isolation with a paravisor-owned partition, on Linux a measured layer below a deprivileged host kernel), and (2) the **GPU offload component**, masked and verified offload for any GPU that does not support, or is not operating in, confidential-computing mode (the card sees masked inputs, returns results verified before use, never receives plaintext protected state). The complete Shield design combines both components on a machine without confidential-computing hardware; neither the name nor one working component establishes the other component's guarantees. On a confidential CPU host whose GPU is outside the confidential boundary, the GPU offload component still applies; a GPU in supported CC mode sits inside the boundary and does not need it. Shield does not give the separate guarantee a confidential processor gives, that the owner cannot inspect data, and copy says so. The GPU rule: within Enclave's architecture Shield's offload is the only supported way to expose a non-CC GPU, on any host; admission reads the card's protection mode from explicit verified evidence, never `teeCpu`, the host OS, whether the CPU is confidential, or the absence of a field, and fails closed. Market copy may state that rule as enforced behavior only for the implementations whose admission checks and evidence establish it; describe the rest as the intended policy. As deployed (2026-10-14) both GPU hosts expose their cards only through the offload: metal0's two V100-class cards (no CC mode) and the Windows Shield host's integrated GPU; and no rebrand may add an unverified admission signal or make current GPU capacity disappear or become newly trusted. Shield is a named component of the market narrative (homepage lede, pillar card, contract card and GPU note, Develop > Architecture section `#shield` with component subsections and `#shielded` / `#masked-offload` alias anchors, host page, ticker, footer), with component-specific availability, evidence limits and trusted parties stated alongside the claim. Lead with the demonstrated protection rather than implementation jargon. Name vendors or platform mechanisms when necessary to explain a trust dependency, compatibility limit or unproven guarantee. Technical detail can live in supporting documentation, but material security limitations must remain visible in public copy.

The adjective "shielded" remains the technical term in code, paths, wire fields and engineering docs for the offload mechanism (the `shielded/` directory, `availability.shielded`, the shielded worker unit and its shared-memory paths, the user-agent string), a compatibility surface never renamed after the brand. Never conflate Shield's product rule with the masked-offload performance research on large models (the mechanism is Shield's, the figures are research and unpublished) or with the orange pVM CPU phone tier.

The phone tier (direction of 2026-09-23): the Pixel TPU path is not a product tier (it demonstrated about 2 tok/s against a 15 tok/s floor for exposure) and TPU or accelerator acceleration is never marketed. Its replacement is the **pVM CPU** tier: CPU-only apps, one protected VM per app, pVM attestation verified at attach, capability-based admission. A Pixel 10 host has served on /enclaves since 2026-10-08 (`avf-pvm-per-app`); it claims no Pixel 11 runtime validation. Its UI identity is the amber ("orange") badge `pvm cpu` everywhere it appears (fleet panel, architecture tab), and it is distinct from the OS-neutral server isolation contract.

Not to be claimed without new evidence: an attested monitor on the IGVM/M3b path (the launch digest covers SVSM and firmware, not the monitor image); M4a as more than one-guest-per-app (it runs in production on metal0 as one SNP guest per app); M4b as measured (in progress); hardware app-vs-app isolation at scale via planes (hardware caps it at 2 to 3 apps per guest); a confidential (CC-mode) GPU host as attached (none is: the NVIDIA CC fleet was retired 2026-10-14, and metal0's cards are outside the boundary, used only through Shield); any public performance number for shielded inference or phone accelerators (each needs model, hardware, protection mode, conditions and checked evidence, and unstable numbers are omitted); permissionless hosting as open today (admission is by a measurement allowlist); Windows hosts as confidential or host-blind; native CreateEnclave as the Windows roadmap (the direction is the partition-per-app backend); a plain or ordinary-virtualization tier as supported, planned, or a fallback.

Constraints future work must respect: the site is static — no server-side rendering or per-user backend for site pages; inline scripts are CSP-hash-synced at deploy (a stale hash silently blocks a page's script); long LLM responses must stream (non-streaming dies at ~180 s at the proxy); custody is limited to the ledger's escrow of the host's share of USDC fundings (owner-refundable while unspent); every contract change is a full ledger migration with a governance (Trezor) cutover, against an EIP-170 size wall with little headroom, and some bindings (bandwidth router, fee router) are one-shot per ledger, so product promises must not assume cheap on-chain iteration.

## Product Principles

1. Explain the privacy outcome together with its scope and trusted parties. Never let a slogan imply a stronger guarantee than the deployment evidence supports.
2. Put material limitations beside each claim, with a link to dated, reproducible evidence. Do not describe a historical result as live proof.
3. Deployer journey first: browse → pay → use. A passkey and a card are the default path and never require crypto knowledge; a wallet is the direct on-chain alternative, offered alongside, never in the way.
4. Anything holding a key is a customer — human or agent; no flow may assume a human. Agents take the wallet and session path; people default to passkey and card.
5. Real proof only: name eyesoff.ai or stage live verification; never manufacture social proof.
6. Protection is a property established by evidence, not a brand or operating-system name. A report proves only what its trust chain, measurement and verified fields establish. A partial implementation must not be sold as satisfying the full contract. Explain Enclave Shield's CPU and GPU components separately, including incomplete validation and the absence of external review.
7. One package, many hosts: portability is a promise about the deployer's build, never about identical guarantees on every backend.
8. Independence is a direction with an honest inventory: name what is already on public contracts and content addressing, name what still runs on company servers, and present the rest as proposals until code establishes it. Existing payments and contracts are not described as being replaced.
