1. On the documented confidential server, the CPU’s AMD SEV-SNP attestation chains to AMD’s hardware root. GPU work uses Shield’s masked offload outside that confidential boundary; no deployed GPU is attesting in NVIDIA confidential mode.

  2. On boot the AMD secure processor signs a report over the launch measurement, a SHA-384 digest of the firmware, the kernel, and the exact runtime image. Change one byte and it changes. Your app is deliberately not in it: the app arrives later by CID, and the runtime refuses any bytes that don't match the CID in your deployment record. (On Intel TDX: a DCAP quote over MRTD + RTMRs.)

  3. The deployed GPUs receive masked inputs through Shield, and the trusted CPU component checks returned results. This is not GPU hardware attestation. Its security depends on the trusted CPU component and Shield’s documented assumptions; review has been internal only. The NVIDIA confidential-GPU fleet was retired on 2026-10-14.

  4. The enclave generates its TLS keypair in sealed memory and folds the public-key hash into the quote's report_data. The private key never leaves the boundary, so the operator can't sit in the middle.

  5. Enclave's source-available verifier (verifier/ in the repo, served from this site) checks it in your browser: it sends the box a fresh nonce, then verifies the signed report up to AMD's root, the firmware floor, the guest policy, the key binding to your nonce and the launch measurement. The API’s selfCheck is only a diagnostic. By default, the expected measurement comes from Enclave’s allowlist, so a successful check is limited. For the confidential-server check, use enclave attest --expect <measurement> with a measurement you derived independently. Pinning does not remove other trust assumptions.
