Security boundaries & verification requirements+

Enclave Shield

Enclave Shield is the protection technology for machines without confidential-computing hardware. Its host isolation component is hardware-enforced per-app CPU isolation: every app runs in its own domain, kept out of the machine's ordinary operating system by a smaller, measured trusted layer that the hardware's virtualization enforces, and the same guarantee holds on every operating system, because the operating system is an implementation backend and never the boundary. The app's identity is measured, its attestation names exactly that identity, its keys and traffic are bound to it, verification fails closed, and a domain that ends leaves nothing behind. There is no software-only fallback. What Shield does not add is the separate guarantee a confidential processor gives, that the machine's owner cannot inspect the data. A host is listed on Shield only after it passes the common acceptance tests.

Confidential hardware

On a confidential processor the same contract gains memory that is encrypted and integrity-protected against the whole machine, operator included, and a report signed by the processor and chained to the silicon vendor that you verify before connecting; TLS terminates inside. Each app keeps its own hardware domain here too; the processor adds the boundary around the whole machine.

GPU admission

Shield's second component is masked, verified GPU offload for any GPU that does not support, or is not operating in, confidential-computing mode. The card sees only masked inputs, returns results that are verified before use, and never receives plaintext protected state. It applies wherever a GPU sits outside the confidential boundary, even when the app runs inside a confidential processor; a GPU operating in a supported confidential-computing mode sits inside the boundary and does not need it. In Enclave's architecture this is the only supported way to expose such a card: admission reads explicit, verified evidence of the card's protection mode, never the CPU, the host, or the absence of a field, and a card outside the boundary is neither advertised nor routed until its Shield evidence verifies. On a machine without confidential-computing hardware both components together are Enclave Shield protection.

Scope and limitations

Nothing here is described as unable to see your data unless hardware enforces it and a verified report says so. A confidential processor defends memory against software, not against someone with physical access and lab equipment on the memory bus, and a self-hosted seller does have physical access to their own machine. A machine that meets part of the isolation contract, a test-signed trusted layer, a key or traffic path that runs through the host, a report signed by software the host controls, is not a level for sale and is not listed. Exactly which hosts are attached, and what each has proven, is the live fleet panel and the dated evidence note on the architecture page, never this paragraph. There is no lower level: ordinary virtualization with the host operating system in charge is a lab baseline, never something we build, list, route work to, or sell. How billing works +

Rates come from the host. Your compute rate reflects your CPU and GPU shares at that host’s posted rates, plus any app publisher fee. Available hardware and capacity vary by host; the deployment flow shows your rate before funding.

Payments buy runtime. Prepaid time is metered by the second. Payments are final and unused time cannot be withdrawn; it stays on the deployment’s public record so you can stop and resume later. Fund in small top-ups.

Resources follow your app. App requirements set minimum allocations. GPU resources require an available GPU host. Resource shares are software-enforced allocations; they do not themselves create hardware isolation. Understand the protection boundary →

The quote includes billing precision. Per-second rates are rounded up to whole millionths of a dollar. Any publisher fee is shown before payment and paid to the app’s publisher.
