Enclave Shielded

The host's operating system cannot see in

Enclave Shielded is our OS-neutral, VBS-like isolation layer for hosts without a confidential-computing TEE. Every app runs in its own hardware-isolated domain, kept out of the machine's ordinary operating system by a smaller, measured trusted layer that the hardware's virtualization enforces. The app's identity is measured, its attestation names exactly that identity, its keys and traffic are bound to it, verification fails closed, and a domain that ends leaves nothing behind. On Windows it maps the contract onto VBS and Hyper-V hardware isolation and the paravisor direction; a Linux backend is part of the product only if it enforces the same contract, and there is no software-only fallback. What it does not add is the separate guarantee a confidential processor gives, that the machine's owner cannot inspect the data. No host is listed on Enclave Shielded until a backend passes the common acceptance tests.

confidential hardware

The machine's operator cannot see in either

On a confidential processor the same contract gains memory that is encrypted and integrity-protected against the whole machine, operator included, and a report signed by the processor and chained to the silicon vendor that you verify before connecting; TLS terminates inside. This is what every host listed today proves. Between apps on one such host, separation is a sandbox and a process while per-app hardware domains are brought to this backend.

GPUs on hosts without a TEE

In Enclave's architecture, Enclave Shielded is the only supported way to expose a GPU on a host without a confidential-computing processor. The card is used without being trusted: inputs are masked before they leave the app's domain and every result is verified before it is used. A host without a TEE neither advertises nor receives GPU work unless the Enclave Shielded contract and its required evidence verify, and admission fails closed.

What we do not claim

Nothing here is described as unable to see your data unless hardware enforces it and a verified report says so. A confidential processor defends memory against software, not against someone with physical access and lab equipment on the memory bus, and a self-hosted seller does have physical access to their own machine. A machine that meets part of the Enclave Shielded contract, a test-signed trusted layer, a key or traffic path that still runs through the host, a report signed by software the host controls, is evidence of work in progress, not a level for sale, and it is not listed as available. There is no lower level: ordinary virtualization with the host operating system in charge is a lab baseline, never something we build, list, route work to, or sell. Deploying is two dials, what you rent, and two rates, what you pay. Prepaying buys runtime by the second; the deployment runs until that time is used up, and you top up to extend it. Payments convert straight into runtime, a paid app's publisher share goes straight to the publisher, and payments are final. Unspent time stays on the deployment's public record (stop now, resume later) but is not withdrawable, so fund in small top-ups. The rates below are the ledger's list rates; a host may post its own, and the store shows the rate of the host your deployment would land on. A GPU share can only be bought while a GPU host is attached, and the live fleet panel is the authority on that.
GPU / VRAM dial × $6/hr per full card

0-100% of one GPU card. VRAM and compute move together: each percent buys ≈1.4 GB VRAM and ≈9.9 TFLOPS. 0% = a CPU-only app.

CPU / RAM dial × $3/hr per whole node

0-100% of the node's vCPU+RAM: each percent buys ≈0.16 vCPU, ≈0.64 GB RAM, ≈10 GFLOPS (a whole node is roughly a thousandth of a card's FLOPS). Your app's memory cap is exactly this slice of the node's 64 GB.

Floors come from app specs minimums, not suggestions

The VRAM, compute, and RAM an app declares in the catalog set each dial's minimum: spec ÷ server spec (140.4 GB / 989 TFLOPS per card; 64 GB / ~1000 GFLOPS per node), larger axis wins, rounded up to a whole percent. An app declaring 494 TFLOPS cannot deploy below a 50% GPU share even if it needs 2 GB of VRAM: you cannot occupy an axis without paying for it.

Rules of the road software caps · two independent dials

The two shares are independent: a GPU app may buy more node than card, and a 100%-GPU, 10%-CPU tenant leaves 90% of that node for CPU-only apps. Unsold CPU on a GPU node is never stranded. Shares are runtime-enforced software caps, not hardware partitions.

price = GPU% × $6/hr  +  CPU% × $3/hr  ·  metered per second

One honest rounding note: the per-second rate is settled in whole millionths of a dollar, rounded up, so the very smallest slices meter a hair above the formula (a 1%-CPU app runs at $0.0324/hr rather than $0.0300). Every estimate shown on the deploy pages already includes this.
